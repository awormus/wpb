Vehicle-borne attacks generally refer to attacks where a vehicle is used as a weapon, either to ram into people or structures, or to carry and deliver explosives. These attacks can be carried out by terrorists or criminals, and can involve various types of vehicles, including cars, vans, and even motorcycles.

Anti-vehicle barriers are crucial for public safety as they protect people and infrastructure from vehicle-based attacks and accidents. These barriers, ranging from bollards to crash-rated barriers, create a physical obstacle that can stop or slow down vehicles, preventing them from reaching intended targets like crowds, buildings, or critical infrastructure.

They are essential in various settings, including airports, government facilities, public venues, and transportation hubs, to mitigate risks associated with vehicle ramming attacks and accidental collisions.

Anti-vehicle barriers are a vital tool for enhancing public safety in various environments. Background Information

The West Palm Beach Police Department (WPBPD) is currently replacing the Police Department Mobile Command Vehicle/Center (MCV).

Currently, the WPBPD operates a 2004-manufactured Ford chassis Winnebago, which is considered end-of-service-life. The current MCV can no longer fulfill the requirements needed when utilized under the nationally recognized Incident Command System (ICS) operational model or support the technological advancements of modern policing in conjunction with the WPBPD Strategic Intelligence Center (STIC). The current MCV is not compatible with retrofitting requirements.

The MCV serves as a unified command hub for all major incidents, including but not limited to (e.g., special events, public demonstrations, natural disasters, weapons of mass destruction, hostage rescue, major crime scenes, and critical incidents).

The MCV should enable coordination with all federal and local response units under the mutual aid agreements and serve as a technological hub for intelligence sources and interoperable radio systems. A modern MCV will provide the WPBPD with the ability to rapidly deploy to any location where the communication infrastructure is inadequate or has been disrupted, or where enhanced interoperability among law enforcement and fire/rescue agencies is needed.

Incident management requires mobility, interoperability among public safety agencies, reliability, and cost effectiveness. In the event the WPBPD and West Palm Beach Fire Rescue (WPBFR) need to conduct public safety operations without access and/or use of police/fire/rescue facilities, the MCV can serve as a temporary operations hub, including an operational dispatch center. A modern MCV can further enhance public safety services when working in tandem with a communications Site on Wheels (SOW). The MCV and SOW allow for the continuation of uninterrupted public safety services, especially during and post-natural disaster incidents, of which South Florida is at high risk of experiencing.

The MCV will also provide an environmentally controlled platform for onscene command and control functions by providing conference areas, video-teleconferencing, digital data transmission and reception, and public information and/or media outlet dissemination. A modern MCV serves this need.

Emergency Vehicles, Inc. (EVI), located at 705 13th Street, Lake Park, Florida 33403, was selected as the vendor for this project.

Background Information

The Department of Children and Families (DCF) has partnered with Southeast Florida Behavioral Health Network Inc. (SEFBHN) to create a state-supported system that would provide a network of publicly funded behavioral health services. The Coordinated Opioid Response Network (CORE) is a state-supported system caring for those suffering from substance use disorder (SUD) and opioid addiction. DCF has allocated opioid settlement funds for the CORE program.

The CORE program focuses on the overall initiative and its goal of coordinating services to address the opioid crisis. It also focuses on recovery and a long-term support system established by the initiative.

This state-wide system delivers comprehensive, long-term care to individuals with substance use disorder, including opioid use. The program connects individuals to immediate and long-term services, including medication-assisted treatment (MAT), mental health support, and recovery resources.

CORE will expand training for first responders, schools, community groups, and families. MAT distribution will increase to non-Medicaid eligible or uninsured individuals in the interim until clients can find a longterm care provider. The program will be used to assist individuals seeking treatment and recovery support services, such as residential and inpatient treatment.

This program allows funding for two (2) Fire Department personnel who will receive specialized training to handle opioid overdoses and support patients with substance use disorders. Training will consist of the administration of MAT and understanding the complexities of addiction.

They will provide counseling, peer support, recovery case management, and residential treatment with access to medications for those who need it with an opioid use disorder (OUD) and any co-occurring SUD and mental health (MH) conditions. Coordinated with hospitals, addiction treatment providers, and other healthcare providers to ensure patients receive comprehensive care. They will also facilitate the transition from emergency care to long-term treatment.

Background Information

The Police Department is requesting approval of Ordinance No. 5142-25 to implement a school zone speeding enforcement program in accordance with Chapter 316, Florida Statutes. The program is intended to further enhance public safety in school zones during the hours of school operations to promote traffic safety and student welfare.

In Florida, school zone speed limits are strictly enforced to protect children. The state has implemented laws authorizing the use of speed cameras to detect and penalize speeding violations in school zones.

School speed zone camera enforcement in Florida utilizes automated systems to detect vehicles exceeding the speed limit within school zones during designated times. These systems, employing fixed-site radar or LiDAR, capture images or videos of the speeding vehicle, including its license plate. Citations are then issued to the registered owner, with the fines typically around $100, and these violations do not incur points on the driver's license.

When a vehicle exceeds the speed limit by a specified margin (often ten [10] miles per hour over), the system captures images or video of the vehicle and its license plate. Law enforcement or a designated authority reviews the evidence to verify the violation. If a violation is confirmed, a citation is mailed to the registered owner of the vehicle. The Police Department reviews each incident and approves the issuance of the notice of violation before it is mailed. Many cities implement a warning or grace period, typically thirty (30) days, where drivers receive warnings instead of citations to encourage compliance. Cameras are typically active thirty (30) minutes before school starts, during school hours, and thirty (30) minutes after school ends.

City Commission - Aug 18 2025

School zone speeding violations in Florida are typically treated as civil infractions, not traffic violations, meaning points are not typically added to the driver's license. The fine for a school zone speeding violation is typically $100. Violators can usually pay the fine online, by mail, or by phone. Individuals can dispute the violation by requesting a hearing or filing a sworn affidavit. Failure to pay the fine or contest the violation may result in a Uniform Traffic Citation (UTC) being issued, which could lead to additional fees and potentially higher fines. The registered owner can request a hearing to challenge a violation notice within thirty (30) days of the notice date. The registered owner can submit a sworn affidavit to establish that a statutory exemption applies, according to Florida Statute § 316.0083(1)(d)1.

The revenue generated from these fines is distributed to various entities, including local municipalities, the state general fund, and local school districts. Fines collected from school zone camera violations can be used to fund school resource officers, active shooter detection systems, and other public safety initiatives.

Studies show that speed cameras have significantly reduced accidents and injuries in school zones by deterring speeding and encouraging safer driving habits. The presence of cameras increases driver awareness of speed limits in school zones. Reduced speeding leads to fewer crashes, injuries, and fatalities involving children.

Many Florida cities and counties have implemented school zone speed camera programs. Some examples include:
